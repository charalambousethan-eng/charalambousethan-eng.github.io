The stove has to get lit

Charlot Magayi’s daughter was two when an open cooking fire burnt her. The poorest, Magayi says, get the most dangerous ways to cook. She opened her talk at the Design Council’s World Design Congress 2025 with that story (Magayi, 2025). I was convinced. Mostly.

Her best line is “a stove unused in a corner changes nothing.” A stove has to fit its kitchen. Papanek (1971) made the same point with a tin-can radio for villages without power. Mukuru Clean Stoves tests in model villages that match real homes, and the women who sell the stoves cook on them too, so they know what fits.

I’d question the fuel. Malaria mosquitoes often bite before bed, when families cook, so Mukuru added a repellent to its fuel. That may be her rule at work: protection from mosquitoes gives families a reason to light the stove. But this talk is about indoor smoke, and one mosquito coil releases as much fine particulate as 75 to 137 cigarettes (Liu et al., 2003). Mukuru’s fuel isn’t a coil, so that settles nothing. I’d like to see the tests.

On a blind sailing week, I helped a man who was profoundly deaf and virtually blind steer a boat. A touch on his left shoulder meant go left, the right meant go right, the middle meant on course. That process is the foundation of VisNav, my haptic wristbands for visually impaired sailors. It fitted him the way her stoves fit kitchens. Will it fit the next sailor?
